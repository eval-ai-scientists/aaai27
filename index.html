Overview

Science at machine speed, judgement still catching up

AI systems have gone from helping scientists with isolated tasks to attempting science itself. A growing family of autonomous research systems now generates hypotheses, designs and runs experiments, and writes up the results with little or no human involvement. What has not kept pace is our ability to judge this work.

The first comparative benchmarks show that the strongest systems score poorly under automated review, that LLM reviewers barely agree with one another, and that coding agents fabricate or fail to validate experimental results in a large share of cases. Human peer review cannot absorb papers produced at machine speed, and we still lack controlled environments in which autonomous scientists can be watched, stress-tested, and compared.

This workshop brings together the communities that must jointly solve this problem, including researchers in agentic AI, evaluation methodology, simulation, AI safety, and the scientific domains themselves. The program features four invited keynotes, contributed paper and poster sessions, the AI Scientist Evaluation Challenge with a real-world drug discovery dataset, and a closing panel asking whether AI can truly judge AI.

Topics of interest

Four themes

We invite submissions on topics including, but not limited to, the four themes below. Work on the human side of this transition is welcome, and position papers that critique current systems and evaluation practice are explicitly encouraged.

01

Evaluating the systems

How capable are AI Scientists, really?

  • End-to-end and stage-wise benchmarks for autonomous research agents, spanning ideation, experimentation, and writing
  • Cost, latency, and resource profiling of autonomous research systems
  • Comparisons across systems and interpretability of AI-generated research decisions
02

Verifying the results

Can we trust what they produce?

  • Reproducibility of AI-generated experiments
  • Hallucination detection and fact-checking in scientific manuscripts
  • Statistical and causal validity of AI-proposed findings
  • Protocols for bringing domain experts into verification
03

Evaluation methodologies

Who, or what, should do the judging?

  • Reliability and calibration of LLM-as-a-judge approaches
  • Multi-model consensus review
  • Human-in-the-loop and human-AI collaborative review
  • Shared metrics for novelty, rigor, clarity, and significance
04

Simulation environments and testbeds

Where can autonomous discovery be studied safely?

  • Agentic simulations of laboratories, clinical settings, and other scientific environments
  • Multi-agent role-play as a stand-in for expert review
  • Transfer of findings from simulated settings to reality
Community effort

The AI Scientist Evaluation Challenge

A challenge organized in collaboration with XtalPi, an AI-driven drug discovery company, built on a real-world dataset from molecular property prediction or drug-target interaction. Results are presented at the workshop, and the annotated corpus is released publicly as a community resource.

Track A

Generation

Participants use an AI Scientist system to produce a research report on the dataset. Submissions are judged by automated metrics and expert reviewers.

Track B

Evaluation

Participants build metrics, frameworks, or tools that assess a provided set of AI-generated papers. Submissions are judged on reliability, scalability, and correlation with expert human judgment.
